We adapt Mixture-of-Recursions to vision: a single shared transformer block applied a variable number of times per token, learning where to spend compute on an image — and across modalities.

Abstract

Standard transformer architectures apply uniform computation to every token, regardless of its semantic complexity. A patch of empty sky is processed by the same number of layers as a patch covering an object boundary — and this asymmetry only grows when several modalities (RGB, depth, normals, text) share a single sequence. Mixture-of-Recursions (MoR) addresses this in language models by reusing a shared block multiple times and learning a per-token routing policy that decides how many times each token is re-processed. Its extension to vision remains largely unexplored.

We adapt MoR to autoregressive image and multimodal generation on CLEVR, building on a SmolLM-135M LLaMA backbone with Middle-Cycle parameter sharing, $N_r{=}3$ recursion steps, and a unified ~242K-token vocabulary covering RGB, depth, normals, and captions. We compare expert-choice and token-choice routers against random-routing and vanilla baselines, and we visualise the learned routing decisions directly on the generated images.

I. Introduction

What is the problem?

Multimodal vision models such as 4M [1,2] and TerraMind [3] process all spatial tokens through the same number of transformer layers; as well as decoder-only approaches like MaskGIT [4]. For example, a sky patch receives the same compute as an object boundary. This uniform computation ignores a key asymmetry: different modalities can carry very different information densities at the same spatial location.

Mixture-of-Recursions (MoR) [5] addresses this in language by learning per-token recursion depth via lightweight routers: a shared parameter block is applied a variable number of times depending on each token's complexity. We propose to extend MoR to vision and multimodal image generation, which leads to the following research questions:

  • RQ1. Does the router learn to assign deeper recursion to semantically complex visual tokens and shallower recursion to simpler ones?
  • RQ2. Does the model allocate computation differently across modalities, and do tokens from different modalities specialize at different recursion depths?
  • RQ3. Can adaptive recursion match or improve generation quality relative to a fixed-depth baseline while reducing compute?

Why is it important?

Efficiency. In the original MoR paper, adaptive recursion achieves lower validation loss with ~50% fewer parameters than vanilla transformers under equal training compute [5]. Extending this to vision, where token sequences are long, could yield significant savings in training time and memory.

Interpretability. Routing patterns provide a direct lens into how the model allocates computation. Visualizing which image patches or modalities receive deeper recursion offers insights unavailable in standard architectures, and mirrors biological vision's selective allocation to salient regions [6].

Generalization to new domains. MoR was introduced in language modeling, and its extension to non-text domains remains largely unexplored. Our work directly tests whether adaptive token-level computation transfers to visual and multimodal generation.

II. Related Work

The Universal Transformer [9] introduced recursive parameter sharing with per-position adaptive halting, showing that different sequence positions can benefit from different effective depths. Mixture-of-Depths (MoD) [10] later proposed top-$k$ routing to skip tokens at individual layers, matching baseline quality with up to 50% fewer FLOPs, but without parameter sharing across layers, so model size still grows with depth. Mixture-of-Recursions (MoR) [5] unifies both ideas by applying lightweight per-token routers to a shared recursive block, establishing a new Pareto frontier across 135M to 1.7B parameter scales. All MoR experiments are conducted on language only, and extending this framework to vision is explicitly listed as an open direction by the authors.

In vision, A-ViT [11] applies per-token adaptive halting to patch embeddings, but the study is restricted to unimodal classification and does not involve parameter-shared recursion. $\gamma$-MoD [12] extends token skipping to multimodal LLMs but relies on unique layers and does not study cross-modal routing patterns. On the generative side, our backbone is informed by any-to-any multimodal generators such as 4M [1,2] and TerraMind [3], which tokenize heterogeneous modalities into a unified discrete vocabulary and train a single transformer to predict across them. We adopt the same recipe, using the Cosmos DI-16x16 tokenizer [7] for visual modalities and GPT-2 for text, but replace the fixed-depth decoder with a recursive one.

To our knowledge, no prior work combines MoR's recursive adaptive-depth framework with discrete multimodal vision tokens or studies cross-modal routing interpretability.

III. Method

Architecture

We build on a SmolLM-135M LLaMA-based [8] decoder-only transformer with Middle-Cycle parameter sharing and $N_r{=}3$ recursion steps. All modalities are mapped into a single discrete token space, so training reduces to standard next-token prediction. Visual modalities are discretized by the Cosmos DI-16×16 tokenizer [7], producing 256 tokens per $256{\times}256$ image, and text uses the GPT-2 BPE tokenizer. The full vocabulary covers roughly 242K tokens, with disjoint codebook slots reserved for RGB, depth, normals, and captions. Token sequences from each modality are concatenated into a single flat sequence following a fixed ordering (RGB, depth, normals, text). A shared recursive block $f_\theta$ is then applied up to $N_r$ times, and a lightweight router decides per token how many recursion steps to take. We use recursion-wise KV caching so that tokens exiting early do not pay attention cost at deeper recursions.

Figure 1. Overview of our MoR-for-Vision architecture. Each modality is tokenized by its own tokenizer and concatenated into a single sequence. A shared recursive block is applied up to $N_r{=}3$ times; the router decides per token whether to continue to the next recursion or exit early.

Two-phase experimental plan

We split our work into two milestones, where findings from the first directly inform the design choices of the second.

Milestone I, unimodal proof of concept. We first train MoR on RGB only, to verify that adaptive recursion transfers from language to vision at all. This phase lets us study routing behavior in a controlled setting, compare expert-choice and token-choice strategies, and tune the key hyperparameters (in particular the residual mixing weight $\alpha$) before adding the complexity of multiple modalities.

Milestone II, multimodal setting. Using the insights from Milestone I, we extend training to RGB, depth, normals, and captions. The router architecture and $\alpha$ value identified in Milestone I carry over directly. Our initial plan called for one dedicated router per modality, but Milestone I results led us to revise this: we now use a single shared router trained with random modality orderings, so that the router sees all modality types in context and cross-modal routing patterns can be studied (RQ2). A per-modality router variant is kept as a planned ablation.

Routing strategies

At each recursion step, the router scores every active token and decides whether it should pass through the block again or exit. We compare two strategies that differ in who makes the routing decision.

Expert-choice routing frames the problem from the recursion stage's perspective: each stage selects the top-$k$ tokens by router score, enforcing a fixed token budget per recursion depth. This guarantees load balancing, but the constraint is purely on count, not on content. In practice we found that the cheapest way for the model to satisfy this budget while minimizing loss is to adopt a positional schedule, routing early-sequence tokens deep and later tokens shallow, regardless of image content (see §IV).

Token-choice routing flips the perspective: each token independently decides its own recursion depth, drawing from a discrete distribution over depths induced by its router scores. There is no fixed budget per stage, so the model is free to allocate depth based on token content rather than position. The router output at each recursion is mixed back into the residual stream with a scalar weight $\alpha$, which controls how strongly the routing decision influences the representation. We found $\alpha{=}1.0$ to be critical: smaller values cause the router to collapse to a single depth, while $\alpha{=}1.0$ rewards differentiation and produces the three-mode depth distribution reported in §IV.

Baselines

To isolate the contribution of learned routing, we compare our model against two baselines.

The vanilla SmolLM-135M is a standard fixed-depth transformer with no recursion and no router, trained under the same conditions. It represents the compute upper bound and the quality target we aim to match or exceed with fewer FLOPs.

The random-router MoR uses the same recursive architecture as our model but samples routing decisions uniformly at random rather than learning them. Comparing against this baseline isolates the contribution of the learned routing policy from the effect of the recursive architecture alone.

IV. Experiments & Results

Setup

All models are trained on the multimodal CLEVR dataset [13] (70K scenes), which provides pre-tokenized RGB, depth, surface normals, scene descriptions, and captions via the Cosmos DI-16×16 tokenizer (256 discrete tokens per visual modality). Milestone I trains on RGB only for 19,500 steps following the original MoR configuration [5]. Milestone II extends training to all four modalities with random ordering. Router analysis

Beyond generation quality, we want to understand what the router actually learns. We look at three complementary views: the average recursion depth it assigns per modality, the full depth distribution within each modality, and a side-by-side comparison with the expert-choice router that collapsed to a positional schedule.

Average recursion depth per modality

We compute, for each output modality, the average recursion depth assigned by the token-choice router, broken down by which modality was used as context at inference time. If the router has learned modality-specific routing strategies, we expect these per-modality averages to differ, both across output modalities and across conditioning modalities (RQ2).

Expert-choice collapse

Although our proposal selected expert-choice routing, it converged in practice to a position-dependent allocation: tokens at early sequence positions received depth 3, and later tokens progressively less, producing the fixed three-band pattern in Figure 5(b) regardless of input content. The reason is structural: expert-choice forces each recursion stage to retain a fixed top-$k$ fraction of tokens, and the cheapest way to satisfy this budget constraint while minimizing loss is to adopt a content-independent positional schedule. We therefore switched to token-choice routing.

(a) Token-choice. Depth-2 in the background, depth-1 around objects, a sparse subset at depth-3.
(b) Expert-choice. A purely positional schedule (early tokens at depth-3, later at depth-1) regardless of image content.

V. Conclusion and Limitations

Summary

This project investigates whether Mixture-of-Recursions, a technique developed for language models, can transfer to vision and multimodal generation. Our Milestone I results on unimodal RGB CLEVR are encouraging: the token-choice router learns a structured, content-dependent depth policy, uses roughly 26% fewer training FLOPs than a vanilla fixed-depth baseline, and produces qualitatively cleaner generations. The routing behavior is interpretable, though it goes against our initial hypothesis: depth concentrates in the background and on a sparse set of register-like tokens, rather than on semantically complex boundaries.

Milestone II extends this to the full multimodal setting, training on RGB, depth, normals, and captions. ...

Limitations

A few important caveats apply to the current results.

  • Scale and domain. All results are at the 135M-base scale on CLEVR, a procedurally generated dataset with strong geometric regularity. It is not clear whether the routing patterns we observe would hold on natural images or at larger scale.
  • Missing compute baseline. A fair answer to RQ3 requires a full-recursion MoR baseline, where every token is always routed to depth $N_r{=}3$, to separate the FLOPs savings of adaptive routing from the effect of the shared architecture alone. This baseline is still being trained.
  • Expert-choice routing. We characterized why expert-choice collapses to a positional schedule on autoregressive sequences, but we did not find a fix for it. Combining its load-balancing guarantees with content-dependence remains an open problem.
  • Multimodal results pending. The cross-modal generation metrics and the router analysis across modalities (Figures 3 and 4) are based on ongoing Milestone II experiments. This page will be updated with final numbers and figures.

Future directions

Two directions stand out for follow-up work. First, scaling: it would be interesting to check whether the inverted depth allocation persists at larger $N_r$, on higher-resolution images, or on natural image datasets. Second, combining MoR routing with modality dropout or classifier-free guidance, to test whether adaptive depth interacts with other forms of conditional compute in a non-trivial way.
