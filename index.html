We adapt Mixture-of-Recursions to vision: a single shared transformer block applied a variable number of times per token, learning where to spend compute on an image — and across modalities.

Abstract

Standard transformer architectures apply uniform computation to every token, regardless of its semantic complexity. A patch of empty sky is processed by the same number of layers as a patch covering an object boundary — and this asymmetry only grows when several modalities (RGB, depth, normals, text) share a single sequence. Mixture-of-Recursions (MoR) addresses this in language models by reusing a shared block multiple times and learning a per-token routing policy that decides how many times each token is re-processed. Its extension to vision remains largely unexplored.

We adapt MoR to autoregressive image and multimodal generation on CLEVR, building on a SmolLM-135M LLaMA backbone with Middle-Cycle parameter sharing, $N_r{=}3$ recursion steps, and a unified ~242K-token vocabulary covering RGB, depth, normals, and captions. We compare expert-choice and token-choice routers against random-routing and full-depth baselines, and we visualise the learned routing decisions directly on the generated images.

On the unimodal RGB setting, the token-choice MoR runs at roughly 26% fewer training FLOPs than a same-architecture vanilla baseline while producing qualitatively cleaner reconstructions. The learned router does not follow our initial hypothesis: it spends deep recursion on a sparse set of register-like tokens, allocates depth-2 to the predictable background, and uses depth-1 around object boundaries — an inverted but interpretable compute allocation. The multimodal extension and cross-modal routing analysis are ongoing.

I. Introduction

The problem

Modern multimodal vision models — from masked encoders like 4M and TerraMind to decoder-only generators like MaskGIT — process every spatial token through the same number of transformer layers. A patch of sky receives the same compute budget as a patch covering an object boundary. The asymmetry is sharper when several modalities are concatenated in a single sequence: a flat-grey region of a depth map and a complex caption token are not equally hard to predict, yet they consume identical compute.

Mixture-of-Recursions (MoR) recently addressed this in language by learning per-token recursion depth: a single shared transformer block is applied a variable number of times depending on a small router's decision. In language, this yields a Pareto improvement over vanilla transformers across 135M–1.7B parameter scales. To the best of our knowledge, the technique has not been studied for vision or multimodal generation. The original authors explicitly list this as an open direction.

Research questions

We organise our work around three questions:

  • RQ1. Does the router learn to assign deeper recursion to semantically complex visual tokens, and shallower recursion to simpler ones?
  • RQ2. Does the model allocate computation differently across modalities? Do tokens from different modalities specialise at different recursion depths?
  • RQ3. Can adaptive recursion match or improve generation quality relative to a fixed-depth baseline while reducing compute?

Why it matters

Efficiency. In language, MoR reaches lower validation loss with roughly half the parameters of a vanilla transformer at equal training compute. Vision token sequences are long — 256 tokens per modality in our setting — so even modest per-token savings compound quickly.

Interpretability. Routing decisions are a direct, faithful window into the model's compute budget. Overlaying recursion depth on generated images gives an interpretability signal that is unavailable in standard architectures and mirrors the selective allocation observed in biological vision.

Generalisation of the technique. Demonstrating that MoR transfers from language to vision (or characterising precisely how and where it fails to) is a contribution in itself, independent of the absolute benchmark numbers we obtain.

II. Related Works

Adaptive computation in transformers

The Universal Transformer introduced recursive parameter sharing with per-position adaptive halting, establishing the idea that different positions may benefit from different effective depths. Mixture-of-Depths (MoD) later proposed top-$k$ routing to skip tokens at individual transformer layers, matching baseline quality with up to 50% fewer FLOPs — but without parameter sharing across layers, so model size grows with depth.

Mixture-of-Recursions (MoR) unifies these two ideas: a single shared block is applied recursively, and a lightweight router decides per token how many recursion steps to take. This combines MoD's per-token compute allocation with the Universal Transformer's parameter sharing, and establishes a new Pareto frontier across 135M–1.7B parameter scales. All MoR experiments are conducted on language only.

Adaptive computation in vision

A-ViT applies per-token adaptive halting to patch embeddings, but the study is restricted to unimodal classification and does not involve parameter-shared recursion. $\gamma$-MoD extends token skipping to multimodal LLMs but relies on unique layers (no parameter sharing) and does not study cross-modal routing patterns. To our knowledge, no prior work combines MoR's recursive adaptive-depth framework with discrete multimodal vision tokens or analyses cross-modal routing interpretability.

Multimodal generative backbones

Our backbone choice is informed by recent any-to-any multimodal generators — 4M, 4M-21, TerraMind — which tokenise heterogeneous modalities into a unified discrete vocabulary and train a single transformer to predict across them. We adopt the same recipe (discrete tokens, single sequence) but replace the fixed-depth decoder with a recursive one, allowing the model to spend variable compute per token and per modality.

Where we sit

Our project is the intersection of these three lines: MoR's recursive shared block, adaptive computation specifically for vision tokens, and multimodal autoregressive generation. We are not aware of prior work that brings the three together, and we treat cross-modal router behaviour as the main interpretability question.

III. Methodology

Architecture overview

Our model is a SmolLM-135M LLaMA-based decoder-only transformer with Middle-Cycle parameter sharing and $N_r{=}3$ recursion steps, fed by a unified multimodal vocabulary of ~242K tokens. The vocabulary reserves disjoint codebook slots for RGB, depth, normals, captions, and scene descriptions. Visual modalities are discretised by the Cosmos DI-16×16 tokenizer (256 tokens per $256{\times}256$ image), and text uses the GPT-2 BPE tokenizer.

Figure 1. Overview of our MoR-for-Vision architecture. Each modality is tokenised by its own tokenizer and concatenated into a single sequence. A shared recursive block is applied up to $N_r{=}3$ times; lightweight routers decide per-token whether to continue to the next recursion or exit early.

Multimodal tokenisation

All modalities are mapped to a single discrete token space so that the model can be trained with vanilla next-token prediction. The token sequences from each modality are concatenated with a fixed ordering at training time (RGB $\rightarrow$ depth $\rightarrow$ normals $\rightarrow$ text) in the proposal; in the multimodal training phase we additionally explore random modality ordering so that the same router can be queried with different modality contexts.

Recursive block and routing

Following the MoR formulation, a single transformer block $f_\theta$ is applied recursively up to $N_r$ times. At each recursion step a small router decides, for every token, whether to continue or exit. The token's final hidden state is the output of its last visited recursion. We implement two routing strategies:

  • Expert-choice routing. At each recursion step, each “expert” (i.e. each recursion stage) keeps the top-$k$ tokens by router score. This was our originally proposed strategy.
  • Token-choice routing. Each token independently chooses its recursion depth from a discrete distribution induced by its router scores. We switched to token-choice after observing that expert-choice collapsed to a positional schedule (see §IV).

The router output at recursion $r$ is mixed back into the residual stream with a scalar weight $\alpha$. We found this $\alpha$ to be a critical knob for stabilisation: too small and the router collapses onto a single depth; with $\alpha{=}1.0$ the router is rewarded for spreading mass across depths and produces a three-mode depth distribution.

We use recursion-wise KV caching so that tokens which exit early do not pay attention cost at deeper recursions.

Per-modality routers (vs. shared router)

Our project proposal called for one router per modality, hypothesising that modalities differ in information density and therefore in optimal recursion depth. Based on the milestone-I findings, we revised this plan: for the multimodal phase we currently use a single shared router fed by random modality orderings, since independent routers cannot share signal across modalities, which would undermine the cross-modal interpretability we want to study (RQ2). The per-modality variant is kept as a planned ablation if time allows.

Baselines

To isolate the contribution of learned routing we compare against:

  • Vanilla SmolLM-135M. Same architecture, no recursion, full fixed-depth forward pass.
  • Random-router MoR. Same recursive block as our model but with routing decisions sampled uniformly at random. This isolates the contribution of the learned policy from the architectural change alone.
  • Full-recursion MoR (in progress). Every token always routed to depth $N_r{=}3$. This is the compute upper bound against which our learned router's FLOPs savings should be measured for a fully controlled answer to RQ3.

IV. Results

Experimental setup

All models in this section are trained on unimodal RGB CLEVR for 19,500 steps under the configuration of the original MoR paper. Reported FLOPs are the total training compute, and the training loss is the standard next-token prediction loss at the end of training. Generations are unconditional, greedy, from a single BOS prompt.

An inverted compute allocation (RQ1)

We initially hypothesised that the router would assign more recursion to semantically complex parts of an image — object boundaries, texture transitions — and less to flat regions. Our preliminary results reject this hypothesis. Figure 2(a) shows the depth heatmap of the trained token-choice router overlaid on a generated CLEVR scene:

  • Depth 2 dominates the background;
  • Depth 1 is concentrated around objects;
  • A sparse subset of 3–5 tokens per image is routed to depth 3.
(a) Token-choice. Depth-2 in the background, depth-1 around objects, a sparse subset at depth-3 (candidate “register” tokens).
(b) Expert-choice collapse. A purely positional schedule (top tokens at depth-3, bottom at depth-1) regardless of image content.

Figure 2. Per-token recursion depth at inference, overlaid on the generated image.

Two effects plausibly explain the inverted pattern:

  • Autoregressive predictability. Given CLEVR's geometric regularity, once an object's boundary is set, its interior becomes highly predictable. Background tokens carry less local entropy individually, but as a group they encode lighting, shadow gradients, and the lookahead for upcoming objects — which appears to justify their slightly deeper recursion.
  • Register-like tokens. The sparse depth-3 set is consistent with the register-token phenomenon recently observed in Vision Transformers, where the model designates low-information positions as global scratchpads. Importantly, these high-recursion tokens do not occur at the same image positions across samples: their location varies per image while their count remains roughly constant — the expected signature of learned, content-conditioned scratchpads rather than a fixed positional artifact. A planned ablation is to mask these tokens at inference and measure the impact on reconstruction quality.

Why expert-choice fails

Although our proposal selected expert-choice routing, in practice it converged to a position-dependent allocation: tokens predicted at early sequence positions received depth-3, and later tokens progressively less, producing the fixed three-band pattern in Figure 2(b) regardless of input image. The reason is structural: expert-choice forces each recursion stage to retain a fixed top-$k$ fraction of tokens. With an autoregressive sequence, the cheapest way for the router to satisfy this constraint while minimising loss is to adopt a content-independent positional schedule. We therefore deviated from the proposal and switched to token-choice routing.

Stabilising the token-choice router: the role of $\alpha$

$\alpha$ is the residual-stream weight applied to deeper-recursion outputs, and therefore controls the expected reward for computing at higher depths. Empirically:

  • Small $\alpha$: the router collapses to predominantly depth-2, producing a near-fixed schedule.
  • $\alpha{=}1.0$: the router is rewarded for differentiating tokens, producing the three-mode distribution that drives the interpretability results above.

V. Conclusion and Limitations

What we have so far

On unimodal RGB CLEVR, MoR transfers from language to vision: the token-choice router learns a structured, content-dependent depth policy and produces qualitatively cleaner generations than both a vanilla fixed-depth baseline and a random-routing ablation, while using roughly 26% fewer training FLOPs than the vanilla baseline. The learned allocation is the opposite of our initial hypothesis — depth concentrates in the background and on a sparse set of register-like positions, not on object boundaries — which is itself an interpretable finding.

Limitations

  • Scale and domain. Our results are at the 135M-base scale on CLEVR, a procedurally generated dataset with strong geometric regularity. A first run on natural images (COCO) was not conclusive: the data pipeline runs, but the model needs adaptation before it produces meaningful samples. We have deferred this until the multimodal CLEVR analysis is complete.
  • Missing baseline. The fairest answer to RQ3 requires a full-recursion MoR baseline (every token at depth 3) to isolate the compute saving from the architectural effect. This is the next baseline to train.
  • Expert-choice is currently a failure case. We characterised why it collapses, but a routing strategy that combines expert-choice's load-balancing with content-dependence would be valuable future work.

Next steps (Milestone II and beyond)

  • Full-recursion baseline (RQ3). Train $N_r{=}3$ MoR with every token routed to depth-3 as the compute upper bound.
  • Cross-modal router analysis (RQ2). Train on RGB, depth, normals, and captions with random modality ordering and a single shared router; measure per-modality entropy and reconstruction quality (FID, SSIM); compare against a per-modality-router ablation.
  • Cross-modal generation. Condition on a subset of input modalities and autoregressively generate the rest (e.g. caption $\rightarrow$ RGB, RGB $\rightarrow$ depth+normals), assessing semantic coherence and the influence of modality ordering at inference.
  • Register-token ablation. Mask the sparse depth-3 tokens at inference and measure the impact on global reconstruction quality, to validate the register-token interpretation.
  • COCO (stretch goal). Revisit natural-image data once the multimodal CLEVR analysis is complete.

Future extensions

Beyond the planned milestones, we see two directions: (i) studying scaling behaviour — whether the inverted depth allocation persists at larger $N_r$ and on higher-resolution images; and (ii) combining MoR routing with classifier-free guidance or modality dropout, to test whether adaptive depth interacts non-trivially with other forms of conditional compute.
