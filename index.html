Abstract

Standard transformer architectures apply uniform computation to all tokens, regardless of their semantic complexity. Mixture-of-Recursions (MoR) addresses this limitation in language by dynamically assigning per-token recursion depth via lightweight routers, but its extension to vision remains largely unexplored. We propose to adapt MoR to multimodal image generation on the CLEVR dataset, investigating whether routers learn semantically meaningful compute allocation across image regions and modalities, and whether adaptive recursion can match generation quality while reducing compute. We show that with $N_r{=}3$ we match feed-forward vanilla transformer in multimodal generation (text-to-image and image-to-text), while using approximately 61% fewer training FLOPs. We additionally show that expert-choice routing, the strategy used in the original language-domain MoR, collapses to a position-dependent depth-allocation in vision, motivating our switch to token-choice. The latter assigns more depths to background tokens than object tokens, contradicting our initial hypothesis. Overall, our results support MoR as a promising efficiency and interpretability mechanism for visual generation, though its effectiveness on downstream applications and natural images remains an open question.

I. Introduction

What is the problem?

Multimodal vision models such as 4M [1,2] and TerraMind [3], as well as decoder-only approaches like MaskGIT [4], process all spatial tokens through the same number of Transformer layers. This uniform computation ignores a key asymmetry: different modalities can carry very different amounts of information densities at the same spatial location. For example, a sky patch receives the same amount of compute as an object boundary.

Mixture-of-Recursions (MoR) [5] addresses this in language by learning per-token recursion depth via lightweight routers: a shared parameter block is applied a variable number of times depending on each token. We propose to extend MoR to vision and multimodal image generation, which leads to the following research questions:

  • RQ1. Does the router learn to assign deeper recursion to semantically complex visual tokens and shallower recursion to simpler ones?
  • RQ2. Does the model allocate computation differently across modalities, and do tokens from different modalities specialize at different recursion depths?
  • RQ3. Can adaptive recursion match or improve generation quality relative to a fixed-depth baseline while reducing compute?

To address this, we tokenize visual and textual modalities into a shared vocabulary and train a recursive decoder with learned token-level depth allocation.

Why is it important?

Efficiency. In the original MoR paper, adaptive recursion achieves lower validation loss with ~50% fewer parameters than vanilla transformers under equal training compute [5]. Extending this to vision, where token sequences are long, could yield significant savings in training time and memory.

Interpretability. Routing patterns provide a direct lens into how the model allocates computation. Visualizing which image patches or modalities receive deeper recursion offers insights unavailable in standard architectures, and mirrors biological vision's selective allocation to salient regions [6].

Generalization to new domains. MoR was introduced in language modeling, and its extension to non-text domains remains largely unexplored. Our work directly tests whether adaptive token-level computation transfers to visual and multimodal generation.

II. Related Work

The Universal Transformer [9] introduced recursive parameter sharing with per-position adaptive halting. Mixture-of-Depths (MoD) [10] later proposed top-$k$ routing to skip tokens at individual layers, matching baseline quality with up to 50% fewer FLOPs, but without parameter sharing across layers. MoR [5] unifies both ideas by applying lightweight per-token routers to a shared recursive block, establishing a new Pareto frontier across 135M to 1.7B parameter scales. All MoR experiments are conducted on language only. In vision, A-ViT [11] applies per-token adaptive halting to patch embeddings for unimodal classification. $\gamma$-MoD [12] extends token skipping to multimodal LLMs but relies on unique layers and does not study cross-modal routing. On the tokenization side, 4M [1,2] and the Cosmos tokenizer [7] provide the discrete multimodal vocabulary we build on, yet all these systems apply uniform computation to every token.

To our knowledge, no prior work combines MoR's recursive adaptive-depth framework with discrete multimodal vision tokens or studies cross-modal routing interpretability.

III. Method

Overview

This section describes the methodological pipeline used in our MoR-for-Vision framework. We first introduce how visual and textual modalities are converted into a shared discrete token space. We then describe the recursive decoder architecture and justify our choice of token-choice routing, based on the limitations observed with expert-choice routing. Finally, we describe autoregressive generation and present the experimental protocol and baselines used to evaluate the contribution of learned adaptive recursion.

Multimodal Tokenization

Visual modalities are discretized by the Cosmos DI-16×16 tokenizer [7], producing 256 tokens per image, and text modalities are tokenized with GPT-2 BPE. All modalities are mapped into a single discrete token space, so training reduces to standard next-token prediction. The resulting unified vocabulary contains 242,271 tokens, with separate codebook slots for RGB, depth, normals, while the text modalities (captions and scene descriptions) share the same codebook slots.

For each sample, token sequences from the modalities are concatenated into a single flat sequence following a fixed ordering (RGB, depth, normals, text). This allows the model to condition on one modality and generate another using the same autoregressive objective.

Recursive Decoder Architecture

We build on a SmolLM-135M LLaMA-based [8] decoder-only transformer with Middle-Cycle parameter sharing. Our main configuration uses $N_r{=}3$ recursion steps, following the original MoR setup [5], and we additionally evaluate an $N_r{=}4$ variant as a recursion-depth ablation. Instead of using a different transformer block at each depth, the model reuses a shared recursive block $f_\theta$ several times. This allows the model to increase the effective computation depth without increasing the number of parameters proportionally.

Routing Strategy: Why We Use Only Token-Choice

We initially considered two routing strategies: expert-choice and token-choice.

Expert-choice routing selects, at each recursion depth, the top-$k$ tokens to process further, enforcing a fixed computational budget per recursion depth. However, we found in the unimodal RGB experiments that the expert-choice router converged to a position-dependent routing pattern: assigning early-sequence tokens deeper recursion steps and later tokens to shallower ones, regardless of the image content (see §IV). We even tested whether this positional collapse could be mitigated by randomly shuffling visual tokens during autoregressive training while retaining their original positional embeddings, inspired by prior work on randomized or order-agnostic autoregressive generation [14][15]. However, the resulting routing behavior remained qualitatively unchanged (see §IV). We therefore switched to token-choice router.

Token-choice routing instead of choosing a fixed number of tokens per recursion depth, each token independently receives a recursion-depth assignment from the start. This gives the model more flexibility to allocate computation based on the token itself, rather than on a global top-$k$ budget. In our experiments, token-choice routing produced a content-dependent depth distribution and was therefore used for the final MoR-for-Vision model.

The router output at each recursion is mixed back into the residual stream with a scalar weight $\alpha$, which controls how strongly the routing decision influences the representation. We found the $\alpha$ parameter to be critical: smaller values (e.g 0.1, 0.5 ) caused the router to collapse to a single depth, while $\alpha{=}1.0$ encouraged differentiation between depth 1, depth 2, and depth 3 tokens (reported in §IV).

Autoregressive Generation

During autoregressive generation, to predict the next token t each recursion step (t), the shared recursive block receives as context all prefix tokens assigned to a recursion depth (\ge t). For the case of three recursion steps, all prefix tokens are processed during the first pass through the shared recursive block. During the second pass, only tokens assigned to depth 2 or 3 are processed again. During the final third pass, only tokens assigned to depth 3 are processed.

After the recursive block, the hidden state is passed to the final transformer layer and then to the Language Modeling head, which outputs 242,271 logits over the shared multimodal vocabulary. The selected token is appended to the sequence and used as context for the next generation step. For visual modalities, the generated discrete tokens are finally decoded back into an image.

Experimental Protocol

We organized the experiments in two stages:

Milestone I, unimodal proof of concept. We first train MoR on RGB only, to verify that adaptive recursion transfers from language to vision at all. This stage lets us test whether adaptive recursion transfers from language to vision, familirize with the codebase, and compare the two different routing strategies (expert-choice vs.token-choice).

Milestone II, multimodal setting. Using the insights from Milestone I, we extend the model to a multimodal setting with RGB, depth, normals, captions and scene description . Our initial plan called for one dedicated router per modality, but Milestone I results led us to revise this. Therefore, we use a single shared router trained with random modality orderings, so that the router sees all modality types in context and cross-modal routing patterns can be studied (RQ2).

Recursion-depth ablation. In addition to our main token-choice MoR model with $N_r{=}3$, we also experimented with a variant using $N_r{=}4$. This ablation allows us to test whether increasing the maximum number of recursive passes improves generation quality. We keep $N_r{=}3$ as the main configuration because it follows the original MoR setup and provides a direct comparison with the baselines, while the $N_r{=}4$ model is used to assess the effect of allowing deeper adaptive computation.

Baselines

To isolate the contribution of learned routing, we compare our model against two baselines.

The vanilla SmolLM-135M baseline is a standard fixed-depth transformer with no recursion and no router, trained under the same conditions. It represents the fixed-compute upper bound and the quality target we aim to match or exceed with fewer FLOPs.

The random-router MoR baseline uses the same recursive architecture as our model but assigns recursion depths randomly instead of learning them. Comparing against this baseline separates the effect of the recursive architecture from the effect of learned routing.

However, this comes at a higher computational cost, reducing the efficiency gain that motivates adaptive recursion relative to $N_r{=}3$.

Expert-choice collapse

Although our proposal selected expert-choice routing, it converged in practice to a position-dependent allocation: tokens at early sequence positions received depth 3, and later tokens progressively less, producing the fixed three-band pattern in Figure 2(b) regardless of input content. The reason is structural: expert-choice forces each recursion stage to retain a fixed top-$k$ fraction of tokens, and the cheapest way to satisfy this budget constraint while minimizing loss is to adopt a content-independent positional schedule. We therefore switched to token-choice routing.

(a) Token-choice. Depth-2 in the background, depth-1 around objects, a sparse subset at depth-3.
(b) Expert-choice. A purely positional schedule (early tokens at depth-3, later at depth-1) regardless of image content.

Perplexity (PPL) measures how uncertain the model is when predicting the next token. Lower perplexity means the model assigns higher probability to the correct next tokens and therefore generates the RGB sequence more confidently. In this RGB-only reconstruction setting, the vanilla fixed-depth model obtains the lowest perplexity. The $N_r{=}3$ token-choice MoR model improves over the random-router baseline, indicating that learned routing is more effective than random recursion-depth assignment. However, increasing the recursion budget to $N_r{=}4$ gives the highest PPL among the tested models. This suggests that allowing deeper recursion is not sufficient to improve generation quality.

Cross-Modal Generation: Quantitative Analysis

We condition the model on one full input modality and generate a target modality autoregressively.

In order to assess visual-to-language and language-to-image generation, as well as structured scene understanding from RGB inputs we restrict our quantitative evaluation to the following setting:

  • RGB → Caption
  • Caption → RGB
  • RGB → Scene description

Different output types call for different metrics:

  • For RGB → Caption generation, we report both CLIPScore and BLEU-4 to capture different aspects of text quality. CLIPScore measures the semantic alignment between the generated caption and the input image. BLEU-4 provides a stricter n-gram overlap measure with the reference caption, allowing us to assess how closely the generated text matches the expected linguistic formulation.
  • For Caption → RGB generation, we use Pixel-MSE and SSIM after decoding the generated visual tokens. Pixel-MSE measures low-level reconstruction error at the pixel level, while SSIM captures perceptual similarity in terms of image structure, contrast, and luminance.
  • For RGB → scene description generation, we evaluate object-level agreement using the F1 score. An object in the generated scene description is considered a match to an object in the ground-truth scene description when their shape, color, and texture all align. For each matched pair, we then compute the L2 distance between the ground-truth position and the generated one.

The $N_r{=}4$ variant performs worse than $N_r{=}3$ on both metrics, again suggesting that additional recursion depth does not automatically translate into better generation quality.

Beyond generation quality, we want to understand what the router actually learns. We look at three complementary views: the average recursion depth it assigns per modality, the full depth distribution within each modality, and a side-by-side comparison with the expert-choice router that collapsed to a positional schedule.

We compute, for each output modality, the average recursion depth assigned by the token-choice router, broken down by which modality was used as context at inference time. If the router has learned modality-specific routing strategies, we expect these per-modality averages to differ, both across output modalities and across conditioning modalities (RQ2).

Comparison with the Original MoR Results

The original MoR paper reports that, in language modeling, MoR with expert-choice routing improves validation loss and few-shot accuracy compared with vanilla Transformers while using substantially fewer parameters or training FLOPs. However, these results are not directly comparable to ours. In our vision setting, expert-choice routing collapsed to a position-dependent schedule, so we focused on token-choice routing instead. This distinction matters because the original authors found token-choice routing to perform worse than expert-choice in their language-modeling experiments (40.0% vs 42.6% on their few-shot benchmark). On the recursion-depth axis, our findings are consistent with theirs: the original paper adopts $N_r{=}3$ as its main configuration across all scales, and our $N_r{=}3$ vs $N_r{=}4$ comparison similarly suggests that increasing the maximum recursion depth from 3 to 4 does not consistently improve generation quality.

Our results partially reproduce the original motivation of MoR, but not the full performance gains. As in the original paper, our token-choice MoR models reduce training compute substantially compared with a vanilla fixed-depth baseline, and the learned router provides interpretable evidence of non-uniform compute allocation. We trained at the 135M scale, the smallest scale tested in the original paper, at which language-domain MoR already outperforms vanilla Transformers. Compute constraints prevented us from training larger variants, so we cannot rule out that the gains reported at 360M to 1.7B in language would also emerge at larger scales for vision. However, at the scale we tested, our MoR models do not consistently outperform vanilla LLaMA on generation quality metrics. This suggests that MoR transfers as an efficiency and interpretability mechanism, but not yet as a direct quality-improving replacement for fixed-depth Transformers in visual generation.

V. Conclusion and Limitations

Summary

This project investigates whether Mixture-of-Recursions, a technique developed for language models, can transfer to vision and multimodal generation. Our Milestone I results on unimodal RGB CLEVR are encouraging: the token-choice router learns a structured, content-dependent depth policy, uses roughly 26% fewer training FLOPs than a vanilla fixed-depth baseline, and produces qualitatively cleaner generations. The routing behavior is interpretable, though it goes against our initial hypothesis: depth concentrates in the background and on a sparse set of register-like tokens, rather than on semantically complex boundaries.

Milestone II extends this to the full multimodal setting, training on RGB, depth, normals, and captions. Although this stage was limited to 5,000 steps due to computational reasons, token-choice MoR remains competitive with the vanilla fixed-depth baseline while using approximately 61% fewer training FLOPs. Cross-modal generation results are mixed, but promising. The vanilla model remains stronger for most generation tasks, with only the caption-to-RGB task as an exception, where token-choice MoR performs best. The $N_r{=}4$ ablation further shows that increasing the maximum recursion depth may improve some aspects of structured prediction, but does not consistently improve generation quality. Together, these results suggest that adaptive recursion can scale to multimodal generation and provide a strong compute-quality trade-off. Overall, our work supports the main motivation of MoR: adaptive recursion can reduce compute and provide interpretable routing. However, it also shows that vision and multimodal generation introduce additional challenges, preventing the same performance gains observed in the original MoR paper for language modeling.

Limitations

  • Scale and domain. All experiments are conducted at the 135M-base scale on CLEVR, a procedurally generated dataset with strong geometric regularity. It is therefore not clear whether the observed routing patterns, including the background-focused depth allocation, would generalize to natural images, higher-resolution inputs or larger models.
  • Generation quality gap with vanilla LLaMA. Although token-choice MoR provides a good compute-quality trade-off, the vanilla fixed-depth LLaMA baseline still outperforms MoR on several generation metrics. The compute savings come at a cost in raw quality, at least at this scale and on this dataset.
  • Limited cross-modal evaluation. Quantitative evaluation is restricted to RGB → Caption, Caption → RGB, and RGB → Scene description. These tasks were selected because they are the most semantically interpretable in our setup, but they do not cover all possible modality pairs.
  • Limited multimodal training budget. The multimodal model was trained for only 5,000 steps due to computational constraints. While the results are promising, longer training would be needed to assess whether MoR can close the remaining gap with the vanilla baseline and whether the learned cross-modal routing patterns remain stable.

Future directions

Several directions stand out for follow-up work. First, our conclusions should be tested at larger scale and on more natural image datasets such as COCO. All current experiments are based on CLEVR and relatively small models, so it remains open whether the observed routing patterns, including the background-focused depth allocation, would persist with larger parameter counts, higher-resolution inputs, or natural image datasets.

Second, future work should explore more sophisticated routing strategies. The objective of current token-choice router is tied to next-token prediction. More sophisticated approaches, such as reinforcement-learning or Q-learning-inspired routing objectives, could explicitly optimize the trade-off between generation quality and computational cost. In addition, we could test a per-modality router variant. This ablation would provide insights into whether RGB, depth, normals, and text benefit from distinct routing policies, or whether a shared router is sufficient to capture cross-modal differences.

Finally, it would be valuable to study MoR with generation frameworks other than autoregressive decoding. Autoregressive generation imposes a sequential order on image tokens, which may be suboptimal for spatial data and can introduce positional biases. Masked-token generation approaches such as MaskGIT could provide a more natural image-generation setting, where tokens are generated or refined in parallel.
